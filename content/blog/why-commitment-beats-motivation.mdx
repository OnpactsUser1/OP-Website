Most fitness plans begin with a burst of motivation. A new month starts, a photo catches you off guard, a friend signs up for a run, and suddenly you're certain: this time it sticks. You buy the shoes. You plan the routine. The first few days feel easy.

Then a Tuesday arrives. You slept badly, work ran late, it's raining, and the feeling that carried you last week is nowhere to be found. Nothing has gone wrong exactly. You just don't *feel* like it.

That Tuesday is where most plans quietly end. Not because people are lazy, but because the plan was built on something that was never designed to last.

## Motivation is weather

Motivation is real, and it's lovely when it shows up. But it behaves like weather. Some days it's bright and warm; other days it's grey and you can't remember why you ever cared. You can't schedule it, and you can't order more of it when you run low.

If your workouts depend on motivation, they'll be as unpredictable as the forecast. You'll have great weeks and empty weeks, and the empty ones will make you doubt the great ones.

The trick isn't to find more motivation. It's to stop needing it.

## Commitment is a decision you make once

A commitment works differently. You make the decision ahead of time, when you're calm and thinking clearly, and then the decision does the heavy lifting on the days you'd rather not.

Compare these two thoughts on a grey Tuesday evening:

- "Do I feel like working out today?"
- "I said I'd do this today, between 6 and 9."

The first question invites a debate you'll often lose. The second isn't a question at all. It's a promise you already made, with a time attached. You don't have to win an argument with yourself; you just have to keep your word.

That shift, from deciding in the moment to deciding in advance, is the whole idea behind OnPacts.

## What a good commitment looks like

Not every promise is a useful one. "I'll get fit this year" is a wish. A commitment you can actually keep has a few qualities.

### It's specific

How many days this week? Which part of the day? What counts as done? OnPacts asks you to choose the number of days you'll work out and a 3-hour time slot for those sessions before your week begins. The vagueness disappears, and so does the wiggle room.

### It's sized for a real week

The best commitment is one you'll keep, not the most impressive one you can imagine. Three days you complete beat five days you abandon on Wednesday. Start smaller than your ambition suggests. You can always raise it next week.

### It's made before the week starts

Plans made on Monday morning, mid-scramble, tend to bend around whatever the day throws at you. Plans made before the week, by the Sunday 11:55 PM deadline in OnPacts, are made by a calmer version of you. Let that version of you do the planning.

### It has a consequence you care about

Commitments get stronger when keeping them matters. In OnPacts, keeping every committed day earns a reward, and missing one means that week doesn't. Nothing is ever taken away, but the week you didn't keep simply doesn't pay. It's a gentle, honest nudge that turns "I should" into "I said I would."

## Why proof matters

There's one more ingredient that makes commitment work: honest feedback. It's easy to remember a week as better than it was. "I mostly worked out" can hide a lot of skipped days.

OnPacts uses your phone's sensors to track and verify each activity, so your history reflects what you really did, not what you hoped you did. That might sound strict, but it's actually freeing. When the record is honest, every completed week means something. You don't have to wonder whether you earned it.

## When motivation does show up

None of this means motivation is useless. When you feel fired up, enjoy it. Use that energy to plan an ambitious-but-realistic week, to try a longer walk, or to invite a friend along.

Just don't build the foundation on it. Let motivation be the bonus, and let commitment be the floor you stand on when the bonus doesn't arrive.

## Start with one pact

If you've started and stopped before, you're in good company. The fix isn't a better pep talk. It's a small, specific promise, made in advance, with a clear way to know whether you kept it.

So pick a number of days you're confident about. Pick the three hours of the day that suit your life. Make the pact before the week begins. Then, on the grey Tuesday, you won't need to feel like it. You'll just need to show up for the pact you made.
